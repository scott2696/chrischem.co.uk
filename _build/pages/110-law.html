Who regulates what, which rules bind operators rather than players, why offshore casinos are lawful for you but not for them, and what has actually changed since the Gambling Act review. Written and fact-checked by our regulation editor.

The short answer

Online gambling is legal in Great Britain for anyone aged 18 or over. The Gambling Act 2005, as amended by the Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014, requires any operator serving British consumers to hold a UK Gambling Commission licence — a duty that falls on the operator, not on you. There is no offence for a British adult who plays at a casino licensed elsewhere, and no UK player has been prosecuted for it. Winnings are entirely tax-free either way. What differs is protection: UKGC sites carry GamStop, a £5 slot stake cap, the credit card ban, affordability checks and free IBAS dispute resolution. Offshore sites carry none of those.

The legal framework

Four instruments do almost all the work:

  • Gambling Act 2005. The foundation. Created the Gambling Commission, established the licensing regime for Great Britain, and set the three statutory licensing objectives: keeping gambling crime-free, ensuring it is fair and open, and protecting children and vulnerable people.
  • Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014. Moved Britain from a "point of supply" to a point of consumption regime. Before it, an operator licensed in Gibraltar or Malta could serve British customers lawfully. After it, any operator transacting with or advertising to consumers in Great Britain needs a UKGC licence regardless of where it is based.
  • Gambling Act 2005 (Operating Licence Conditions and Codes of Practice) — the LCCP. The Commission's rulebook, and where most of the practical requirements actually live: GamStop integration, customer fund disclosure, marketing rules, KYC timing, and responsible gambling tooling.
  • The Gambling Act Review and subsequent measures. The 2023 white paper set the current direction of travel: online slot stake limits, financial vulnerability checks, a statutory levy on operator revenue, and stronger game design rules. These have been implemented in stages.

Northern Ireland is separate, governed by the Betting, Gaming, Lotteries and Amusements (Northern Ireland) Order 1985 — see below.

What the Gambling Commission does

The UKGC licenses and regulates commercial gambling in Great Britain. In practice it:

  • Issues operating licences to operators, personal management licences to senior staff, and premises licences via local authorities
  • Sets and enforces the LCCP
  • Investigates breaches and imposes financial penalties — regulatory settlements running to tens of millions of pounds are routine
  • Maintains a public register of licensees, which anyone can search
  • Regulates the National Lottery

What it does not do, and this matters:

  • It does not regulate the stakes a bookmaker chooses to accept from an individual. Restricting a winning punter's account to pennies is lawful and outside the Commission's remit. This is the most common complaint about UK bookmakers and the Commission cannot help with it.
  • It does not adjudicate individual disputes. That is IBAS or another approved ADR provider.
  • It has no jurisdiction over offshore operators beyond pursuing them for unlicensed supply.

The 2025–26 reforms, with dates

The Gambling Act review has been implemented in stages rather than in one instrument. These are the measures now in force, with the dates that matter.

Online slot stake limits — 9 April and 21 May 2025

A maximum stake of £5 per spin for players aged 25 and over took effect on 9 April 2025, and £2 per spin for players aged 18 to 24 on 21 May 2025. Introduced by statutory instrument following the 2023 white paper. It applies to online slots at UKGC-licensed operators only, not to live dealer, table games or sports betting.

The statutory levy — 6 April 2025

Licensed operators pay a compulsory levy of up to 1.1% of gross gambling yield, collected by the Gambling Commission and ringfenced for research, prevention and treatment. It was expected to raise about £100 million a year and brought in roughly £120 million in year one, allocated 50% to the NHS-led treatment system, 30% to prevention and 20% to UK Research and Innovation. It replaced the previous voluntary donation arrangement.

Bonus and promotion rules — 19 January 2026

The Commission announced on 26 March 2025 an amendment to Social Responsibility Code Provision 5.1.1 (Rewards and bonuses), in force from 19 January 2026. Three substantive changes:

  • Wagering requirements capped at 10 times the bonus. The Commission's own example: a £10 bonus at 50x requires £500 of play, which is now prohibited.
  • Wagering applies to bonus funds only — deposit-plus-bonus wagering is no longer permitted.
  • Mixed-product incentives banned — no bonus conditional on gambling across two or more product types.

Financial vulnerability checks

Light-touch checks run on publicly available data at defined deposit thresholds, designed to identify players in financial difficulty without requiring documents from the majority. A pilot of enhanced assessments for higher-spending accounts has run alongside.

What this means for the offshore comparison

The bonus reform inverts a long-standing claim

Offshore casinos have historically been said to offer better bonuses. Since 19 January 2026 that is only true of size. On terms, a UKGC-licensed site is capped at 10x on bonus funds, while the offshore operators we list run 25x to 35x and some apply the multiple to deposit plus bonus. Who regulates what

The Gambling Commission

Licenses and regulates commercial gambling in Great Britain, enforces the LCCP, issues financial penalties, and maintains a public register of licensees. It also regulates the National Lottery.

Local licensing authorities

Issue premises licences for betting shops, casinos, bingo halls and arcades under delegated powers. Nothing to do with online play.

IBAS and approved ADR providers

Adjudicate individual disputes with UKGC licensees, free to the customer, with the operator bound by the outcome. Not available for offshore operators.

The Advertising Standards Authority

Enforces the CAP and BCAP codes on gambling advertising, including the rules on appeal to under-18s and on the presentation of offers.

HMRC

Collects Remote Gaming Duty, General Betting Duty and the other gambling duties from operators. Has no interest in your winnings.

The Information Commissioner's Office

Handles data protection complaints, including how an operator has handled your identity documents.

Who regulates nothing here

No UK body regulates an offshore operator's terms, adjudicates your dispute with it, or can compel it to pay you. That asymmetry is the single most important practical fact on this page.

Offences under the Gambling Act, and who commits them

Section 33 — providing facilities for gambling

Committed by an operator that provides remote gambling facilities to consumers in Great Britain without a UKGC licence. Punishable by fine and imprisonment. Committed by the operator, not the player.

Section 330 — advertising unlawful gambling

Committed by anyone advertising gambling that would constitute an offence under section 33. This is why offshore brands do not appear on British television or radio.

Sections 46 and 47 — inviting or permitting a child to gamble

Committed by operators and by adults who facilitate underage gambling. The minimum age is 18.

Section 42 — cheating

Committed by a player who cheats at gambling, which includes actual or attempted deception in relation to a game. Bonus abuse is a contractual matter rather than a criminal one, but collusion and device manipulation are not.

What is not an offence

Playing at a casino licensed outside Great Britain. There is no provision creating such an offence, no prosecution has ever been brought against a UK player for it, and winnings remain tax-free.

What is still coming

The gambling ombudsman

The 2023 white paper proposed a single ombudsman for gambling disputes, intended to replace the patchwork of ADR providers with one route. Implementation has been industry-led and is still bedding in.

Further affordability work

The Commission has continued to develop frictionless financial risk assessments, with the stated aim of catching the highest-risk accounts without imposing document checks on ordinary players.

Continued enforcement against unlicensed operators

The Commission works with payment providers, hosting companies and search engines to disrupt operators serving Great Britain without a licence. The practical consequence for a player is that an offshore site can become unreachable at short notice — another reason not to leave a balance on one.

What the law asks of you

Remarkably little. As a player in Great Britain you must:

  • Be 18 or over. Gambling under 18 is an offence, and accounts opened with false details are closed with winnings forfeited.
  • Give accurate details. Providing false information to obtain gambling services can amount to fraud, quite apart from voiding your account.
  • Not gamble with the proceeds of crime. The Proceeds of Crime Act applies here as everywhere.

That is essentially the list. There is no requirement to use a UK-licensed operator, no offence in playing offshore, no declaration to make, and no tax to pay.

The offshore position, precisely

This is where most guides become vague, so here it is properly.

For you

Lawful. The Gambling Act 2005 creates offences for providing facilities for gambling without a licence, not for using them. No provision makes it an offence for a British adult to gamble at a foreign-licensed site, and there has never been a prosecution of a player for doing so. Your winnings are tax-free under the same rules that apply to a UK-licensed bet.

For the operator

Not lawful, if it is targeting Great Britain. Under section 33 of the Gambling Act 2005, providing facilities for remote gambling to consumers in Great Britain without a UKGC operating licence is a criminal offence. Section 330 covers advertising unlawful gambling. Offshore operators generally take the position that they serve a global market rather than targeting Britain specifically; the Commission takes a different view and pursues enforcement where it can, including through payment providers, hosting services and ISP blocking requests.

The practical consequence for you

Not legal jeopardy — commercial risk. An offshore site serving UK players can become unavailable at short notice if enforcement action lands, its payment routes are cut, or it simply decides Britain is not worth the exposure. Balances held at that moment are difficult to recover, because customer funds are rarely segregated and you have no ombudsman. Withdraw regularly and keep balances small. That single habit removes most of the real-world risk.

GamStop and self-exclusion in law

GamStop is operated by the not-for-profit National Online Self-Exclusion Scheme Limited. Since 31 March 2020, integration has been a licence condition under the LCCP for every UKGC remote licensee. Registration blocks all UK-licensed gambling sites and apps for six months, one year or five years, and it cannot be reversed before the term expires. Reinstatement afterwards requires an active request plus a 24-hour cooling-off period.

Because it is a UKGC licence condition, it binds only UKGC licensees. An offshore operator has no legal obligation to consult the register and no technical means of doing so. That is the entire mechanism behind "non-GamStop" sites — a jurisdictional gap, not a loophole someone engineered.

Separate schemes cover land-based gambling: the Multi-Operator Self-Exclusion Scheme for betting shops, SENSE for land-based casinos, and equivalents for bingo and arcades. Registering with one does not register you with the others.

Advertising rules

  • CAP and BCAP codes prohibit gambling advertising that appeals strongly to under-18s, portrays gambling as a solution to financial problems, or suggests it enhances personal qualities.
  • The whistle-to-whistle ban, industry-led since 2019, removes gambling adverts from live sport broadcasts from five minutes before kick-off to five minutes after the final whistle, before the 9pm watershed.
  • Affiliate marketing is caught: a UKGC licensee is responsible for the compliance of any affiliate promoting it, which is why licensed-brand affiliate pages carry heavy standardised disclaimers.
  • Free bet and bonus advertising must state significant conditions with the offer, not only in linked terms.

None of this binds an offshore operator, which is why you will not see these brands advertised on British television or radio, and why their promotional wording is less constrained than a UK brand's.

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

  • England, Scotland and Wales form "Great Britain" for gambling purposes and share the Gambling Act 2005 and the UKGC. Gambling regulation is reserved to Westminster, though Scotland and Wales have devolved powers over some aspects of premises licensing planning.
  • Northern Ireland is regulated separately under the Betting, Gaming, Lotteries and Amusements (Northern Ireland) Order 1985, as amended in 2022. The UKGC has no jurisdiction there. In practice most UK-licensed online operators accept Northern Irish customers, and the online position for a player is materially similar.

Your rights when something goes wrong

At a UK-licensed operator

  1. Complain to the operator using its published procedure. It must respond within eight weeks.
  2. Escalate to the ADR provider — usually IBAS, sometimes eCOGRA. Free to you, and the operator is bound by the decision.
  3. Report to the Gambling Commission. It will not resolve your individual case but it uses complaints as regulatory intelligence, and patterns lead to enforcement.
  4. Financial Ombudsman, where the dispute concerns a payment service rather than the gambling itself.

At an offshore operator

  1. Complain to the operator in writing, citing the specific clause relied on.
  2. Complain to the licensing regulator — the Curaçao Gaming Control Board or the Anjouan Gaming Authority. Response times run to weeks and outcomes are not guaranteed.
  3. Use a mediation service. AskGamblers, Casino Guru and ThePOGG have real leverage with reputation-sensitive operators, and in practice resolve more offshore disputes than regulators do.
  4. Chargeback, if you funded by debit card. Contentious for gambling transactions and likely to close your account. Crypto has no equivalent.

There is no IBAS route, no Gambling Commission complaint and no Financial Ombudsman route for an unlicensed operator. That asymmetry is the single most important practical difference between the two markets.

UK gambling law FAQs

Is online gambling legal in the UK?

Yes, for anyone aged 18 or over. The Gambling Act 2005 and the Gambling (Licensing and Advertising) Act 2014 require any operator serving British consumers to hold a UK Gambling Commission licence, but that duty falls on the operator rather than the player. Playing at a site licensed elsewhere is not an offence for you and never has been.

Is it illegal to play at a casino without a UK licence?

Not for you. For the operator it is: section 33 of the Gambling Act 2005 makes it an offence to provide facilities for remote gambling to consumers in Great Britain without a UKGC licence. No UK player has been prosecuted for playing at an offshore site, and winnings remain tax-free. The practical risk to you is commercial rather than legal — a site can become unavailable at short notice, which is why you should withdraw regularly.

What is the legal gambling age in the UK?

18 for all commercial gambling in Great Britain, including online casinos, sports betting, bingo and the National Lottery. Every operator runs age and identity verification, and accounts opened with false details are closed with winnings forfeited. Gambling under 18 is an offence.

What is the £5 slot stake limit?

A cap on the maximum stake per spin at UK Gambling Commission licensed online casinos, set at £5 for players aged 25 and over and £2 for those aged 18 to 24. It came out of the 2023 Gambling Act review. It binds UKGC licensees only, so offshore casinos are not subject to it — which is one of the main practical differences between the two markets.

Why are credit cards banned for gambling in the UK?

The Gambling Commission banned them at licensed operators on 14 April 2020 after finding credit card gambling was strongly associated with harm, particularly among those already in financial difficulty. Offshore sites may still accept them, but your card issuer treats a gambling transaction as a cash advance, applying a fee and charging interest from day one with no interest-free period.

When did the UK cap casino bonus wagering at 10x?

19 January 2026, under an amendment to Social Responsibility Code Provision 5.1.1 announced by the Gambling Commission on 26 March 2025. UK-licensed operators may now apply a wagering requirement of no more than ten times the bonus, must apply it to bonus funds only rather than deposit plus bonus, and may no longer offer mixed-product incentives requiring play across two gambling types. It binds UKGC licensees only, not offshore operators.

What is the statutory gambling levy?

A compulsory charge on licensed operators of up to 1.1% of gross gambling yield, in force since 6 April 2025 and collected by the Gambling Commission. It replaced the previous voluntary donation system and is ringfenced for research, prevention and treatment of gambling harm — 50% to the NHS-led treatment system, 30% to prevention and 20% to UK Research and Innovation. It raised roughly £120 million in its first year. Offshore operators contribute nothing.

What offence does an offshore casino commit by serving UK players?

Section 33 of the Gambling Act 2005 makes it an offence to provide facilities for remote gambling to consumers in Great Britain without a UK Gambling Commission licence, and section 330 makes it an offence to advertise such gambling. Both are committed by the operator, not by you. No provision creates an offence for a British adult who plays at a foreign-licensed site, and no such prosecution has ever been brought.

Is a gambling ombudsman being introduced?

The 2023 white paper proposed a single gambling ombudsman to replace the current patchwork of approved alternative dispute resolution providers, giving consumers one clear escalation route. Implementation has been industry-led and is still settling. It would apply to UK Gambling Commission licensees only — an offshore operator would remain outside it, as it is outside IBAS today.

Does the Gambling Commission regulate offshore casinos?

No. Its jurisdiction covers licensees and the enforcement of unlicensed supply. It can pursue an offshore operator for serving Great Britain without a licence, including through payment providers and ISP blocking requests, but it cannot regulate that operator's terms, adjudicate your dispute with it, or require it to pay you.

Can the Gambling Commission help if a bookmaker restricts my account?

No. Restricting or closing an account, and setting maximum stakes for commercial reasons, are business decisions outside the Commission's remit. This is entirely lawful and is the most common complaint about UK bookmakers. It is also the main legitimate reason experienced punters use offshore books, which restrict far less aggressively.

Is gambling law different in Northern Ireland?

Yes. Northern Ireland is regulated under the Betting, Gaming, Lotteries and Amusements (Northern Ireland) Order 1985, as amended in 2022, and the UK Gambling Commission has no jurisdiction there. In practice most UK-licensed online operators accept Northern Irish customers and the online experience for a player is materially similar to Great Britain.

What is IBAS and can I use it?

The Independent Betting Adjudication Service, the main approved alternative dispute resolution provider for UK-licensed gambling operators. It adjudicates disputes free of charge to the customer and the operator is bound by the outcome. It is only available where the operator holds a UKGC licence — there is no equivalent route at an offshore casino or bookmaker.
